# Index Guidance — How to Read Your Vocabulary

A teaching companion to the Index reference page. The Index reference tells you *what* the panel does. This document tells you *what to look for, why it matters, and how to act on what you see.*

---

## §1 · The Index is a diagnostic instrument, not a file finder

A traditional file index answers: *"Which files contain this word?"*

The Constellation Index answers: *"What concepts inhabit my knowledge system, in what proportions, and where do they connect?"*

The shift is subtle but consequential. When you open the Index you're not looking for a specific note — you're looking at the **shape** of your thinking. Every term the Index lists is a slot in your conceptual vocabulary. The frequency next to each term is the gravity of that concept in your library. The "also appears with" chip strip beneath each expanded term is the **cognitive adjacency** — the words your mind reaches for in the same breath.

Approach the Index the way a botanist approaches a soil sample. You're cataloging what grew, in what density, with what neighbors. From that you infer what the soil is good for.

---

## §2 · Three reads — what to look at, in what order

### Read 1: The frequency profile

Open the Index. Switch sort to **frequency** (most-common first). Scroll the top 20 terms.

These are your **load-bearing concepts** — the ideas your library is structurally about. Don't read them as topics. Read them as **the questions you've been answering**, the lens you've been looking through, the language you've been thinking in.

**What to ask:**
- Are the top-20 words the words I *thought* my library was about? If yes — your stated concerns and your actual writing align. If no — the gap is where the real work is. Either your stated topic is wrong, or your writing has drifted.
- Is the top-1 word a 10× spike over top-2? That's a **monoculture** — useful sometimes (when you're deep on one project) but worth noticing if it persists. A healthy intellectual life has 3–5 co-equal top concepts, not a single one swallowing everything.
- Is there a Wikipedia-style metadata word in the top-10 ("source", "license", "wikipedia", "isbn")? That's a signal you're cataloging *about* knowledge rather than *building* knowledge. Both are valid; just know which mode you're in.

### Read 2: The Arabic ↔ English balance (or any language pair)

Switch the script tab to **عربي**. Note the term count above the list.
Switch back to **All**. Note the new count. Subtract: that's your non-Arabic vocabulary.
Switch to **English** (or your other primary language). Note its count.

**What to look at:**
- The ratio. If you write in two languages but one's vocabulary is 5× the other's, your **conceptual richness** is uneven across languages. You may be a sophisticated thinker in Arabic and a literal note-taker in English (or vice versa). The Index is showing you which language carries your thinking.
- The cross-language toggle (Settings → Index → Expand mentions cross-language). Turn it on. Click a top Arabic term — do English notes surface with "via {lemma}" badges? If yes, your two language-halves are talking to each other. If no, you're effectively running two parallel libraries that never meet. Whether that's a feature or a bug is yours to decide.

### Read 3: The cognitive adjacency

Pick a term you care about. Expand it. Read the **"Also appears with"** chips below the mentions list.

These are the words that **co-occur** with your chosen term across your notes. Think of them as the company your concept keeps. The chips are ranked by note-count; the strongest associations come first.

**What to look at:**
- Are the chips the *expected* neighbors, or surprising ones? Surprises are the most valuable read. If you click "knowledge" and see "manuscript" / "transmission" / "isnad" — that tells you your "knowledge" concept is shaped by an Islamic-tradition lens. Click "knowledge" and see "data" / "model" / "training" — different lens entirely. Same word, different conceptual home.
- Are the chips trending toward **objects** or toward **operations**? Object-heavy ("book", "library", "manuscript") suggests static cataloging. Operation-heavy ("derive", "compare", "synthesize", "argue") suggests active thinking. Neither is wrong; both are diagnostic.
- A chip you don't recognize — that's a **cross-pollination opportunity**. Click it. You'll often find a thread of your thinking you'd half-forgotten.

## §3 · Common patterns and what they mean

### Pattern: One term has 5–10× the count of any other

**Reading**: monoculture / project-mode. You're deep in one thing.
**Action**: if intentional, fine. If not, you may be over-indexing one project at the expense of broader vocabulary growth.

### Pattern: Top-20 is dominated by metadata words ("source", "wikipedia", "isbn", "abstract")

**Reading**: catalog mode. Your library is mostly *imported* knowledge with attribution metadata.
**Action**: this is fine for a reference library. It becomes a signal when it's the *whole* library — meaning you've cataloged extensively but synthesized rarely. The Sight panel shows whether the metadata-heavy notes are isolated nodes or genuinely connected.

### Pattern: Cross-language toggle on, but no badges anywhere on common terms

**Reading**: your library is monolingual at the **concept** level even if you've written in multiple languages. Each language operates in its own conceptual sandbox.
**Action**: identify the cross-lingual concepts you care about (e.g. "knowledge" / "علم", "tree" / "شجرة"). When writing about them, mention the cross-language equivalent at least once in the body. The Lexical Bridge will then surface those notes for you across both languages on subsequent searches.

### Pattern: A high-frequency term has very thin "Also appears with" chips

**Reading**: the term is widely used but in the same context — it's a cliché in your library, not a connector.
**Action**: if the term is core to your work, this is a flag. Real intellectual concepts grow in many directions. A term that always appears with the same 3–4 neighbors is functioning as a label, not a thinking tool.

### Pattern: You expand a term and the cross-language toggle surfaces an unexpected language

**Reading**: gold. The Lexical Bridge has identified a translation in your library you didn't know you had — likely an imported source you forgot existed.
**Action**: read the surfaced note. Cross-pollination opportunity from your past self.

## §4 · The Index as a writing prompt

Beyond reading, the Index is a writing prompt:

- **Empty slots**: open the Lens or Sight panel side-by-side. Find a high-frequency Index term. Ask: *"Where in the Lens / Sight does this concept live?"* Often the answer is "everywhere" or "nowhere" — both are interesting starting points.
- **Adjacency stretch**: pick a top term, write a note that bridges it to the LEAST-common chip in its "Also appears with" list. Forces a connection that doesn't exist yet.
- **Bilingual bridging**: with the cross-language toggle on, find a term where badges appear. Write a note explicitly stating the equivalence — *"The English word X corresponds to the Arabic concept Y, but with the difference Z."* Articulating the translation is itself a knowledge act.

## §5 · What the Index will NOT tell you

The Index is a frequency + co-occurrence diagnostic. It's not a semantic engine. Specifically:

- **It doesn't know meaning.** "bank" (river) and "bank" (financial) appear as one term. The Lexical Bridge only kicks in cross-language; same-language polysemy is invisible.
- **It can't tell you what's important.** Frequency is a signal of attention, not value. Your most important note may use its term once.
- **It can't tell you what's missing.** A concept that should exist but doesn't will simply not appear. Use the Lens or a directed read for that.
- **It doesn't synthesize.** Two terms with high co-occurrence might be deeply connected or just clichéd neighbors. The Index reports the pattern; you interpret the meaning.

The Index pairs well with the Sight panel (which shows topology), the Sky View (which shows wikilink graph), and the Map (which shows hierarchy). Each instrument answers a different question; the Index answers *the vocabulary question.*

## §6 · A weekly practice

If you're using Constellation seriously, a 10-minute weekly Index review is high-leverage:

1. **Open Index, sort by frequency.**
2. **Read the top 20 terms.** Has the list shifted from last week? What's new in the top 20? What dropped out?
3. **Pick one term whose count moved meaningfully.** Expand it. Read the "Also appears with" chips. Does the adjacency tell you something about how your thinking has shifted?
4. **If you maintain in two languages**, switch script tabs. Compare top-20 between languages — are you developing the same concepts in both, or specializing?
5. **Toggle cross-language expansion on.** Click your week's most-frequent term. Are there notes in the other language about the same concept? Surprises live here.

Five minutes of looking is worth an hour of reading any single note. The Index is the meta-view; treat it as one.
