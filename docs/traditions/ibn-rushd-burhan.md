# Ibn Rushd burhān (مراتب البرهان)

**Family**: Arabic / Islamic beyond uṣūl · **Shape**: rings (4 concentric zones)

## Hero metaphor

The dome divides into **four concentric rings** of discourse, in
descending order of demonstrative force from center to rim:

- **burhān** — apodictic demonstration. The philosophers' rigor.
- **jadal** — dialectic. The theologians' contention from probable
  premises.
- **khaṭāba** — rhetoric. The preachers' and jurists' persuasion.
- **shiʿr** — poetic image. The discourse of imaginative association
  for the general public.

This is Ibn Rushd's adaptation of Aristotle's *Organon* — *Posterior
Analytics*, *Topics*, *Rhetoric*, *Poetics* mapped to four audiences.
Each is the *correct* discourse for its hearer; the visualization is
not a hierarchy of better vs. worse but of *fitness to audience*.

Constellation defaults notes to *shiʿr* (the outermost ring) on the
defensible reading that most knowledge begins as imaginative
association before being elevated through dialectic to demonstration.

## Scope

**When to use this tradition.** When working with philosophical,
theological, rhetorical, or pedagogical content where the same idea
takes different discourse-forms for different audiences. Useful for
analyzing rhetoric in religious-philosophical texts, for teaching the
classical hierarchy of arguments, and for self-audit (am I writing in
*burhān* mode or *khaṭāba* mode?).

**When NOT to use this tradition.** When content has no
audience-discourse dimension — pure technical content, descriptive
empirical work, and creative work outside the philosophical-rhetorical
register fit poorly.

## Applicability

- Falsafa and kalām analysis.
- Rhetoric and audience analysis in religious texts.
- Teaching the four-discourse hierarchy as a writing self-audit.

## Lineage

12th-century Andalusian falsafa. Ibn Rushd (Averroes, 1126–1198) of
Córdoba, in the *Faṣl al-Maqāl* (Decisive Treatise) and his
commentaries on Aristotle's *Organon*, adapted the Greek discourse
hierarchy to the Islamic context — arguing that *burhān* is the
philosophers' privileged mode but does not delegitimize *jadal*,
*khaṭāba*, or *shiʿr* for their proper audiences. The treatise was a
direct intervention in the post-Ghazālī polemics about whether
philosophy was permissible in Islam.

## Critique

Ibn Rushd's hierarchy has been criticized as elitist — placing
philosophers' demonstration above the masses' poetic-imaginative
understanding can read as condescension. Modern Muslim philosophers
have variously defended the framework (as honest about audience
diversity) and critiqued it (as a residue of Greek aristocratic
intellectualism). The Constellation rendering uses a concentric-ring
shape, which makes the hierarchical reading visually salient — users
who reject the hierarchy may prefer to read the rings as discourse
*kinds* rather than as a quality ladder.
