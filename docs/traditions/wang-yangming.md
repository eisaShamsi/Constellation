# Wang Yangming (王陽明)

**Family**: East Asian Confucian · **Shape**: binary-flow (vertical, with central liángzhī)

## Hero metaphor

The dome divides into **two hemispheres**, left and right, with
bidirectional flow arrows connecting them and a central labeled axis:

- **zhī** 知 (knowing) — one hemisphere
- **xíng** 行 (acting) — the other hemisphere
- **liángzhī** 良知 (innate moral knowing) — the central axis labeled
  as the reservoir that feeds both

Wang Yangming's distinctive Ming-dynasty doctrine — *zhī-xíng héyī*
(知行合一, the unity of knowledge and action) — is rendered as a
*binary with flow* rather than as two separate cells: the two are
analytically distinct but in practice one. The bidirectional arrows
say: every knowing is already an acting, and every acting is already a
knowing.

The vertical-layout binary-flow renderer was added in Phase η.2
specifically for this tradition, distinct from the horizontal-layout
binary-flow used by Ibn Khaldūn and the concentric-layout used by
Dussel.

## Scope

**When to use this tradition.** When your content explicitly connects
insight to practice — when the question is "what did this knowing
*do*?" or "what knowing is implicit in this act?". Useful for
applied-ethics, contemplative-practice, and any writing where the
divorce of theory from practice is what you want to refuse.

**When NOT to use this tradition.** Purely theoretical content where
the action-dimension does not arise, and purely practical content
where the knowing-dimension is taken for granted, fit poorly. The
framework is also strongly anti-dualist — it does not accommodate the
ordinary distinction between "I know X" and "I sometimes fail to act
on X."

## Applicability

- Wang Yangming studies and Ming Neo-Confucian philosophy.
- Applied ethics where action-implicit-in-knowing is the point.
- Contemplative-practice journaling that resists the
  knowledge/practice split.

## Lineage

Ming-dynasty Neo-Confucianism. Wáng Yángmíng (王陽明, 1472–1529), a
Ming statesman, general, and philosopher, founded the *Xīnxué*
(心學, School of the Heart-Mind) as a critical alternative to the
Zhu Xi orthodoxy (Cheng-Zhu Lǐxué, 理學) that dominated official
Confucianism. Wang's claim that knowledge and action are one
challenged the Zhu Xi position that one first investigates the
principles of things (gewu) and then acts on what one knows. The
doctrine was both philosophically radical and politically
consequential — Wang's followers became a major intellectual force in
late-Ming China and in early-modern Japan and Korea.

## Critique

The unity thesis has been criticized as philosophically obscure —
critics within and outside the tradition ask how one accounts for
genuine *akrasia* (acting against what one knows is right). Wang's
defenders argue the doctrine *redescribes* akrasia rather than
denying it: what looks like knowing-without-acting is actually
incomplete knowing. The Constellation rendering as a flow-binary
respects the analytical distinction Wang refused to collapse
entirely.
