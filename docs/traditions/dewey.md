# Dewey

**Family**: Modern Western · **Shape**: cyclic-flow (5 segments + arrow)

## Hero metaphor

The dome is rendered as a **cyclic ring with five segments** — Dewey's
**pattern of inquiry** — with chevron arrows indicating the
clockwise temporal flow. The five stages, in order:

1. **Indeterminate situation** — the unsettled, problematic, doubtful
   condition that initiates inquiry.
2. **Problem formulation** — naming what is wrong, framing what is at
   stake.
3. **Hypothesis (idea)** — proposing a possible resolution.
4. **Reasoning** — working out the implications of the hypothesis.
5. **Testing / judgment** — checking the hypothesis against the
   situation; arriving at a (provisional) determinate situation.

The cycle then re-fires from a new indeterminate situation. The
chevron arrows make the *temporality* visible: this is not a static
typology but a process.

## Scope

**When to use this tradition.** When your universe is itself a record
of *inquiry* — research notes, experimental notebooks, debugging logs,
investigative journalism, casework. Visualizing notes by their stage in
the inquiry cycle surfaces where you are stuck (problem formulation?
hypothesis-poor?), where work piles up (always testing, never
re-framing?).

**When NOT to use this tradition.** Static, encyclopedic, or
descriptive content does not fit a pattern-of-inquiry frame — there is
no inquiry to be at-a-stage-of. Reference notes, defined-term notes,
and contemplative writing fit poorly.

## Applicability

- Research notebooks where each note carries an inquiry-stage tag.
- Debugging, troubleshooting, and root-cause analysis logs.
- Pedagogical analysis of how investigation actually unfolds.

## Lineage

American pragmatism, mature phase. John Dewey (1859–1952) articulated
the pattern of inquiry as the systematic core of his *Logic: The
Theory of Inquiry* (1938). The pattern is intended as a universal
description of how settled belief is recovered from unsettled doubt —
applicable equally in everyday problem-solving, scientific
investigation, ethical deliberation, and aesthetic judgment.

## Critique

The pattern has been criticized as too tidy — actual inquiry rarely
proceeds through five discrete stages in clean sequence. Dewey himself
qualified the pattern as an analytical reconstruction, not a
descriptive sequence. From the philosophy-of-science side, the pattern
overlaps with hypothetico-deductive and abductive models; from the
phenomenological side, it can flatten the lived experience of
inquiry's surprises and dead-ends.

## Citation

**Primary.** John Dewey, *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (New York:
Henry Holt, 1938), esp. ch. 6 ("The Pattern of Inquiry").

**Modern.** Larry A. Hickman, *John Dewey's Pragmatic Technology*
(Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 1990); Matthew J. Brown,
*Science and Moral Imagination: A New Ideal for Values in Science*
(Pittsburgh: University of Pittsburgh Press, 2020).
