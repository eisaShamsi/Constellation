# Shāṭibī maqāṣid (مقاصد الشريعة)

**Family**: Arabic / Islamic beyond uṣūl · **Shape**: grid (3 tiers × 5 sectors = 15 cells)

## Hero metaphor

The dome divides into a **3 × 5 grid** — three concentric tiers crossed
by five radial sectors — yielding 15 cells, one per (tier, necessity)
combination.

The three tiers, innermost to outermost:

- **ḍarūriyyāt** — essentials. Without these the human order collapses.
- **ḥājiyyāt** — needs. These ease and improve life but are not
  strictly required for survival.
- **taḥsīniyyāt** — embellishments. The refinements of conduct and
  culture.

The five sectors (al-kulliyyāt al-khams, the five necessities):

- **dīn** — religion
- **nafs** — life
- **ʿaql** — mind
- **nasl** — lineage
- **māl** — property

The sectors are rotated +π/4 from the cardinal axes (the Longino
pattern) so dividers land at NE/SE/SW/NW positions, clearing the
vertical axis from stratum-label collision.

## Scope

**When to use this tradition.** When working with legal, ethical, or
policy content that maps onto the *maqāṣid* framework — questions
about which goods are at stake (life, mind, religion, lineage,
property) and at what tier of necessity (essential, needed,
embellishing). The 15-cell grid is the cleanest geometric
representation of any Islamic register in the curated set.

**When NOT to use this tradition.** Non-legal-ethical content doesn't
sort meaningfully into the 15 cells. The Shāṭibī framework is also
deliberately deontological-teleological — it presupposes that goods
can be ranked, which some critical traditions reject.

## Applicability

- Sunni *uṣūl al-fiqh* and *maqāṣid*-based legal-ethical reasoning.
- Contemporary applied ethics in Islamic finance, environmental
  policy, bioethics.
- Public-policy analysis using a goods-and-tiers framework.

## Lineage

14th-century Maliki Andalusian *uṣūl al-fiqh*. Abū Isḥāq al-Shāṭibī
(d. 790/1388) of Granada, in *al-Muwāfaqāt fī uṣūl al-sharīʿa*, gave
the *maqāṣid* their most systematic medieval treatment, building on
al-Juwaynī (d. 478/1085) and al-Ghazālī (d. 505/1111). The framework
has had a major contemporary revival through Jasser Auda and others,
who apply it to systems thinking, public policy, and Islamic
finance.

## Critique

The neat 3 × 5 = 15 cells can mislead — al-Shāṭibī's actual treatment
is more porous than the grid suggests, with cross-tier and
cross-necessity considerations throughout. The contemporary maqāṣid
revival has been criticized (from within the tradition) for risking
the reduction of *uṣūl al-fiqh* to a utilitarian calculus; from
outside, for inheriting deontological assumptions that not all moral
traditions share.
