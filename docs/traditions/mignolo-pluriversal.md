# Mignolo pluriversal

**Family**: Latin American decolonial · **Shape**: relational (hub-and-spoke)

## Hero metaphor

The dome shows a **central modernity disc surrounded by multiple
satellite clusters**, with spoke lines linking them — Mignolo's
*pluriversality* / *pensamiento fronterizo* (border thinking) rendered
as a hub-and-spoke network. The center is the modern/colonial European
center; the satellites are the *border epistemes*, the standpoints
from which the center can be seen *as* a particular position rather
than as the unmarked universal.

Constellation's curated rendering uses Mignolo's own decolonial
vocabulary for the satellite cluster labels (rather than invoking
specific indigenous traditions, per the religious-lineage rule and
the broader v2.10 blanket-exclusion ruling for Indigenous frames):
*colonial difference*, *epistemic disobedience*, *border gnosis*,
*pluri-versal*, and *de-westernizing*. The cluster set is the
geometric scaffold for the pluralism Mignolo argues for — the chip is
*about* irreducible plurality, not a claim to have catalogued it.

## Scope

**When to use this tradition.** When you want a *meta*-register for
your work — a frame that reminds you any one of the curated
traditions is itself situated, that there is always a *from-where* to
your sorting. Useful for critical theory, comparative philosophy
that takes its own situatedness as a topic, and any project where the
question "from where is this true?" is a question your project takes
seriously.

**When NOT to use this tradition.** Pluriversal as a *primary* sorting
of content is somewhat self-undermining — Mignolo himself critiques
the move to "put everything into a register." Useful as a discipline,
inappropriate as a daily-driver lens. Honest framing: this is a
register that asks you whether registers themselves are the right
move.

## Applicability

- Decolonial-theory writing and its self-reflection.
- Comparative philosophy taking its own situatedness as a topic.
- Critical analysis of any taxonomy or controlled vocabulary.

## Lineage

Latin American decolonial theory. Walter D. Mignolo (b. 1941,
Argentina; later at Duke University), influenced by Aníbal Quijano's
*coloniality of power* concept, developed *border thinking* and
*pluriversality* across *Local Histories / Global Designs* (2000) and
*The Darker Side of Western Modernity* (2011). The framework is part
of the modernity/coloniality group's broader critique of Eurocentric
universalism.

## Critique

Putting Mignolo "inside" a register is *performative tension*: his
work explicitly critiques the gesture by which the European center
catalogues subaltern knowledges. Constellation's hub-and-spoke
geometry can read as enacting the very gesture Mignolo critiques. The
honest move is to offer the chip as a *prompt to suspicion* — it is
the only chip in the curated set whose use is partially a critique of
the chip-set's existence.

The pluriversal framework has also been criticized from within
decolonial theory itself (e.g., by Catherine Walsh) for being
insufficiently grounded in specific subaltern struggles. Constellation
does not adjudicate this dispute; the chip is the framework as
Mignolo articulates it, with its own critiques surfaced honestly.

## Citation

**Primary.** Walter D. Mignolo, *Local Histories / Global Designs:
Coloniality, Subaltern Knowledges, and Border Thinking* (Princeton:
Princeton University Press, 2000; 2nd ed. 2012); *The Darker Side of
Western Modernity: Global Futures, Decolonial Options* (Durham: Duke
University Press, 2011).

**Modern.** Walter D. Mignolo and Catherine E. Walsh, *On
Decoloniality: Concepts, Analytics, Praxis* (Durham: Duke University
Press, 2018); Arturo Escobar, *Designs for the Pluriverse: Radical
Interdependence, Autonomy, and the Making of Worlds* (Durham: Duke
University Press, 2018).
