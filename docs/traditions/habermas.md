# Habermas

**Family**: Modern Western · **Shape**: sectoral (3 wedges)

## Hero metaphor

The dome divides into **three knowledge-constitutive interests** —
Habermas's claim that knowledge production is always already shaped by
one of three irreducible human interests:

- **Technical** — instrumental control of objectified processes. The
  empirical-analytic sciences.
- **Practical** — intersubjective understanding through interpretation.
  The historical-hermeneutic sciences.
- **Emancipatory** — self-reflection toward autonomy (*Mündigkeit*).
  The critical sciences.

The three sectors sit at 120° each, rotated +π/6 (§δ.1-fix-1) to keep
dividers off the stratum labels at the top of the dome.

## Scope

**When to use this tradition.** When the question is *what is this
knowledge for?* — what interest underlies its production. Useful for
diagnosing scholarly mix in a research universe: are you doing
technical work, hermeneutic work, or critical work? The emancipatory
slice in particular is a good visual prompt to ask whether your project
includes self-reflection on its own conditions.

**When NOT to use this tradition.** Habermas's interests classify the
*purpose* of knowing, not the *content*. If your notes are sorted by
topic rather than by the work they do, this tradition surfaces little.
Also a heavy tradition to apply naïvely; the three-fold division has
been substantially complicated by Habermas's own later
*communicative-action* framework.

## Applicability

- Methodology and philosophy of social science.
- Critical-theory work and its self-reflection.
- Distinguishing applied / interpretive / critical research strands in
  a single project.

## Lineage

German critical theory, second-generation Frankfurt School. Jürgen
Habermas (b. 1929) articulated the three knowledge-constitutive
interests in *Erkenntnis und Interesse* (1968) as the explicit
framework for his early epistemology. The interests were later
subsumed under the *Theory of Communicative Action* (1981) — but as a
typology of inquiry-stance the 1968 formulation remains pedagogically
useful and is what Constellation renders.

## Critique

Habermas himself moved away from the strict three-interest typology in
his mature work; critics (including Rorty, Foucault from a different
angle) argued the typology essentialized interests that are in practice
more fluid. The emancipatory interest has been the most contested —
critics ask whether it is genuinely distinct from the practical
interest's hermeneutic critique. Constellation ships the 1968 typology
because it is geometrically clean; users wanting the mature
*communicative-action* framing would need a separate tradition.

## Citation

**Primary.** Jürgen Habermas, *Erkenntnis und Interesse* (Frankfurt am
Main: Suhrkamp, 1968); English: *Knowledge and Human Interests*, trans.
Jeremy J. Shapiro (Boston: Beacon Press, 1971).

**Modern.** Thomas McCarthy, *The Critical Theory of Jürgen Habermas*
(Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1978); James Bohman, *Public Deliberation:
Pluralism, Complexity, and Democracy* (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1996).
