# Maimonidean prophecy (Mishnah ha-Nevuʾah)

**Family**: Jewish (Abrahamic) · **Shape**: ladder (11-step spiral)

## Hero metaphor

The dome shows **eleven graduated steps** of prophetic knowledge,
rendered as a spiral ladder rising from the rim inward. The lower steps
are accessible to the philosopher who has perfected reason and
imagination but has not received divine emanation; the higher steps
involve increasing direct contact with the Active Intellect. The
specific identity of each step is intentionally not shown in the chip —
the *Guide of the Perplexed* II.45 enumerates them as the textual
authority. Above the eleventh step sits a (visually unrepresented)
twelfth: the prophecy of Moses, which Maimonides treats as a special
kind beyond the hierarchy.

The spiral renderer was added in Phase ζ.2 specifically to honor
hierarchical-N-step traditions that don't fit a clean sectoral
geometry.

## Scope

**When to use this tradition.** When your content has a genuinely
graduated structure (whether literally about prophecy or by
methodological analogy — degrees of insight, stages of mastery,
levels of contemplative attainment). Useful for medieval Jewish
philosophical content and for projects that explicitly use a
graduated-attainment vocabulary.

**When NOT to use this tradition.** When content has no hierarchical
structure of the right shape, the eleven-step ladder will dominate
visually without surfacing meaningful pattern. Eleven is a high
cardinality — most user content will cluster at one or two steps.

## Applicability

- Medieval Jewish philosophy and the falsafa-influenced rationalist
  tradition.
- Comparative analysis of prophecy across the Abrahamic religions.
- Graduated-attainment frameworks more generally (Buddhist *bhūmis*,
  Sufi *maqāmāt*) — though those are not part of the curated baseline.

## Lineage

12th-century Andalusian-Egyptian Jewish philosophy. Moses Maimonides
(Rambam, 1138–1204), in the *Moreh Nevukhim* (Guide of the Perplexed),
part II, chs. 32–48, articulated the eleven levels as a synthesis of
biblical-prophetic material with Avicennan and Aristotelian
philosophy. The hierarchy was a controversial reading in its own time
(Maimonides was attacked from both rationalist and traditionalist
sides) and remains a touchstone in medieval-Jewish-philosophy
scholarship.

## Critique

The eleven-step hierarchy presupposes the medieval Aristotelian
philosophy-of-mind that grounded it (Active Intellect, perfection of
imagination as a precondition for prophecy). Modern readers without
that framework can find the steps hard to motivate. The specific
ordering has also been disputed within the Maimonidean commentary
tradition. The hierarchy is also philosophical-elite-oriented: the
practical question "did this prophet speak truly?" is not addressed
by the hierarchy of *how* prophecy is received.

## Citation

**Primary.** Moses Maimonides, *Moreh Nevukhim* (Guide of the
Perplexed), part II, chs. 32–48. English: Shlomo Pines, trans., *The
Guide of the Perplexed*, 2 vols. (Chicago: University of Chicago
Press, 1963).

**Modern.** Howard Kreisel, *Prophecy: The History of an Idea in
Medieval Jewish Philosophy* (Dordrecht: Springer, 2001); Kenneth
Seeskin, *Maimonides on the Origin of the World* (Cambridge:
Cambridge University Press, 2005).
