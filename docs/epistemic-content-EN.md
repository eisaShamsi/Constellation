# Epistemic Content: A Comparative Civilizational Survey

*A scholarly essay drawing together the world's major epistemological traditions on the genus of cognitive objects.*

---

## Introduction

Whenever human beings speak of "things in the mind" — ideas, facts, data, knowledge, beliefs, theories — they presuppose a single overarching category: the class of *epistemic content* (المحتوى المعرفي, *al-muḥtawā al-maʿrifī*). Epistemic content names whatever can serve as the object of cognition, communication, or knowing — whatever a mind can grasp, hold, transmit, or evaluate as true or false. It is the genus of which idea, fact, data, and knowledge are species.

Although the modern phrase "epistemic content" emerged in twentieth-century analytic philosophy, the underlying concept — a structured family of cognitive objects, sources, and states — was independently developed in at least five major civilizations: Greek and post-Greek European thought, Arabic-Islamic *kalām* (theology) and *falsafa* (philosophy), Indian *pramāṇa* (means-of-knowledge) theory, Chinese Mohist and Neo-Confucian thought, and the Persian-Islamic Illuminationist (*Ishrāqī*) tradition that fused inheritances from both Iran and Greece. African, Jewish, and indigenous American traditions also contributed distinctive frames. This essay surveys those civilizational treatments, identifies their convergences, and traces the lineaments of a genuinely cross-cultural taxonomy.

A note on method. The essay treats each tradition on its own terms first — using its native vocabulary, paired with English equivalents — before drawing comparisons. The aim is not to flatten the traditions into a single Western frame, but to show that they were addressing the same underlying terrain from different vantage points, and that any complete account of epistemic content today must acknowledge all of them.

## I. The Greek and Western European tradition

Western treatments begin with Plato's *Theaetetus* (c. 369 BCE), where Socrates considers — and tentatively rejects — the proposal that knowledge (ἐπιστήμη, *epistēmē*) is true belief with a *logos* (account or justification). This formulation became the seed of the Justified True Belief (JTB) analysis that dominated Western epistemology for two and a half millennia, surviving Edmund Gettier's famous 1963 counterexamples in *Analysis* 23(6) only as a starting point for refinement (Gettier, 1963; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy [SEP], "The Analysis of Knowledge").

Aristotle's *De Anima* and *Posterior Analytics* added the architecture: sensation (αἴσθησις, *aisthēsis*) feeds memory, memory feeds experience (ἐμπειρία, *empeiria*), and experience yields *epistēmē*, the demonstrative knowledge of necessary causes. Above *epistēmē* sits *nous* (νοῦς), the intuitive grasp of first principles; alongside it stand *technē* (τέχνη, productive craft) and *phronēsis* (φρόνησις, practical wisdom). Aristotle thus already distinguishes sources of knowledge from epistemic states, and both from higher syntheses — the same three loci that recur in every later mature tradition.

The Stoics introduced the influential notion of the *lekton* (λεκτόν, "what is said") — the propositional content that statements express, distinct from the words that express it and from the things words denote. The *lekton* anticipates modern conceptions of propositional content (Frege's *Gedanke*, Russell's proposition) by some two thousand years.

Medieval scholastics — Albertus Magnus, Aquinas, Scotus, Ockham — systematized the Aristotelian inheritance through the doctrine of *intentiones*, distinguishing first intentions (about things) from second intentions (about concepts). Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781/1787) reorganized the field around the active synthesizing mind: sensory intuition (*Anschauung*) supplies content, the categories of the understanding supply form, and knowledge arises only at their intersection.

Twentieth-century analytic epistemology, after Frege's distinction of sense (*Sinn*) and reference (*Bedeutung*) and Russell's analysis of propositions, developed elaborate taxonomies: propositional knowledge (knowledge-*that*), procedural knowledge (knowledge-*how*), acquaintance knowledge (knowledge-*of*), tacit knowledge (Polanyi, 1958), and the Data-Information-Knowledge-Wisdom (DIKW) hierarchy popularized by Russell L. Ackoff (1989) within information science.

## II. The Arabic-Islamic Sunni tradition

Sunni Islamic epistemology developed across three overlapping disciplines: *kalām* (الكلام, theology), *uṣūl al-fiqh* (أصول الفقه, legal-theoretical methodology), and *falsafa* (فلسفة, philosophy in the Greek-influenced tradition). The result is one of the most finely graded vocabularies for epistemic content ever produced.

In *kalām* and *uṣūl*, the master category is *al-idrāk* (الإدراك, apprehension or cognition). Al-Jurjānī's *Kitāb al-Taʿrīfāt* (الكتاب التعريفات, fifteenth century) divides it cleanly into two: *taṣawwur* (تصور, simple conception of a meaning without judgment) and *taṣdīq* (تصديق, judgment or assent to a proposition). This binary maps almost exactly onto the modern distinction between concept and proposition.

Above *taṣdīq* lies a graded scale of epistemic states: from *shakk* (شك, equipoised doubt) through *ẓann* (ظن, preponderant probable opinion — the standard epistemic status of much *fiqh*) and *iʿtiqād* (اعتقاد, settled belief) to *ʿilm* (علم, knowledge) and *yaqīn* (يقين, certainty). The *uṣūlī* tradition mapped this scale precisely onto sources of legal authority: *mutawātir* (متواتر, mass-transmitted) reports yield *yaqīn*, while *āḥād* (آحاد, solitary) reports yield *ẓann*. Below *taṣdīq* lie defective states: *jahl basīṭ* (simple ignorance — knowing that one does not know), *jahl murakkab* (compound ignorance — believing falsehood to be true), and *wahm* (وهم, illusion or false supposition).

The recognized sources of knowledge (*masādir al-maʿrifah*, مصادر المعرفة) in mainstream Sunni epistemology are five: *al-ḥiss* (الحس, sensation), *al-ʿaql* (العقل, reason), *al-khabar al-ṣādiq* (الخبر الصادق, reliable testimony — comprising both *al-naql al-ṣaḥīḥ* / authentic transmission of revelation and well-witnessed factual reports), *al-tawātur* (التواتر, mass-transmission yielding necessary knowledge), and for many scholars *al-fiṭrah* (الفطرة, innate disposition) or *al-ilhām* (الإلهام, inspiration).

The *falsafa* tradition — al-Fārābī (d. 950), Ibn Sīnā / Avicenna (d. 1037), Ibn Rushd / Averroes (d. 1198) — added Aristotelian apparatus: the *maʿqūlāt* (معقولات, intelligibles), abstracted from sensory particulars by the active intellect (*al-ʿaql al-faʿʿāl*); the explicit *taṣawwur–taṣdīq* distinction reflecting Aristotle's *Posterior Analytics*; and the doctrine of *yaqīn* as demonstrated knowledge of necessary causes. Al-Ghazālī (d. 1111), in *al-Munqidh min al-Ḍalāl* and *Miʿyār al-ʿIlm*, mapped epistemic states to their certifying processes, arguing that only *ʿilm yaqīnī* — knowledge whose negation is inconceivable — counts as genuine knowledge.

Persian-Islamic Illuminationism (*ḥikmat al-ishrāq*, حكمة الإشراق), founded by Shihāb al-Dīn al-Suhrawardī (d. 1191), introduced a distinction now widely cited: *al-ʿilm al-ḥuṣūlī* (العلم الحصولي, acquired or representational knowledge — knowledge through a mental form that stands for its object) versus *al-ʿilm al-ḥuḍūrī* (العلم الحضوري, presential knowledge — direct, non-representational acquaintance, as in self-knowledge). This anticipates by centuries Bertrand Russell's distinction between knowledge by description and knowledge by acquaintance (Russell, 1912).

## III. Indian traditions: the *pramāṇa* framework

Classical Indian philosophy developed perhaps the most rigorous taxonomy of epistemic content, organized around three master concepts: *pramā* (प्रमा, valid cognition or knowledge), *prameya* (प्रमेय, the object of cognition), and *pramāṇa* (प्रमाण, the means or instrument by which valid cognition is produced).

The *pramāṇa* framework canonized six possible sources of valid knowledge: (1) *pratyakṣa* (प्रत्यक्ष, direct perception); (2) *anumāna* (अनुमान, inference); (3) *upamāna* (उपमान, comparison or analogy); (4) *śabda* (शब्द, verbal testimony, especially of reliable authorities including scripture); (5) *arthāpatti* (अर्थापत्ति, postulation, inference to the best explanation); and (6) *anupalabdhi* (अनुपलब्धि, non-apprehension or knowledge of absence).

Different schools accepted different subsets, and these differences map onto deep metaphysical commitments:

- *Cārvāka* (materialist): *pratyakṣa* alone.
- *Vaiśeṣika* and Buddhist schools (Dignāga, Dharmakīrti): perception and inference only — comparison and testimony reducing to inference.
- *Sāṃkhya* and *Yoga*: perception, inference, testimony.
- *Jaina*: perception, inference, testimony — paired with the famous *anekāntavāda* doctrine of multi-perspectival truth.
- *Nyāya* (founded by Akṣapāda Gautama, *Nyāya-Sūtras* c. second century BCE): four — perception, inference, comparison, testimony.
- *Mīmāṃsā* and *Advaita Vedānta*: all six.

Buddhist epistemology, formalized by Dignāga (c. 480–540 CE) in the *Pramāṇasamuccaya* and refined by Dharmakīrti (c. 600–660 CE) in the *Pramāṇavārttika* and *Nyāyabindu*, became one of the world's most sophisticated logico-epistemological systems. It distinguished *svalakṣaṇa* (the unique particular, object of perception) from *sāmānyalakṣaṇa* (the conceptual universal, object of inference), and argued that only direct perception of unique particulars yields ultimate truth (*paramārtha-satya*), while conceptual cognition gives only conventional truth (*saṃvṛti-satya*).

Indian epistemology also developed a refined typology of cognitive contents: *jñāna* (ज्ञान, cognition in general), *prama* (valid cognition), *aprama* (invalid cognition — including *saṃśaya* / doubt, *viparyaya* / error, and *tarka* / hypothetical reasoning), *smṛti* (memory), and *anubhava* (immediate experience). The Naiyāyikas pioneered a theory of cognitive error (*khyātivāda*) and a procedural account of inference (the five-step *pañcāvayava* syllogism) that bears comparison with Aristotelian and modern formal logic.

## IV. Chinese traditions

The most rigorous classical Chinese epistemology comes from the later *Mohist Canon* (墨經, *Mò Jīng*), the technical chapters of the *Mozi* compiled in the late fourth century BCE and rediscovered as a coherent logico-epistemic system only in the twentieth century. Together with Aristotle's *Organon* and Indian *Hetuvidyā*, the *Mohist Canon* is regarded as one of the three classical systems of world logic (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Mohist Canons").

The Mohists classified knowledge (*zhī* 知) by source into three types: (1) 聞知 *wén zhī* — knowledge by hearing or testimony; (2) 說知 *shuō zhī* — knowledge by explanation or inference; and (3) 親知 *qīn zhī* — knowledge by direct personal acquaintance. They organized this around the principle of *míng-shí* (名實) — correspondence between *name* (linguistic sign) and *reality* (its object) — and developed canonical procedures of *biàn* (辯, argumentation), *lèi* (類, sortal-similarity or analogical extension), and *gù* (故, reason or cause).

Confucian epistemology, especially in the *Analects* and the *Mencius*, treated *zhī* as inseparable from moral self-cultivation: to know the Way (*dào* 道) is to walk it. Mencius held that humans possess innate moral knowledge — the four "sprouts" (*sì duān* 四端) of compassion, shame, deference, and discrimination — that need only be cultivated.

Daoist epistemology, especially in the *Zhuangzi* (fourth–third centuries BCE), pressed a radical perspectivism: every standpoint sees only its own view of things, and the names and distinctions humans impose on reality may distort the *dào* itself. Zhuangzi's "transformation of things" (*wù huà* 物化) anticipated by two millennia modern arguments about the constructed character of conceptual schemes.

Neo-Confucianism systematized epistemology around the slogan *gé wù zhì zhī* (格物致知, "investigation of things, extension of knowledge") drawn from the *Great Learning*. Zhū Xī (1130–1200) read this as a programme of empirical and reflective inquiry into the patterns (*lǐ* 理) immanent in things. Wáng Yángmíng (1472–1529) read it instead as turning inward to the *liángzhī* (良知, innate moral knowledge) and reformulated the relation as *zhī xíng hé yī* (知行合一, the unity of knowing and acting): genuine knowledge already includes its own enactment, and a "knowledge" that does not act is no knowledge at all.

## V. Other civilizational traditions

**Jewish epistemology** developed in deep dialogue with the Greek and Arabic-Islamic traditions. Maimonides (Moshe ben Maimon, d. 1204), in *Guide of the Perplexed*, adapted Avicennian apparatus for Jewish theology, distinguishing demonstrative *ʿilm* from *ẓann* and framing the limits of human knowledge of God in terms strikingly parallel to Sunni *kalām*. The Talmudic tradition itself preserved a finely graded vocabulary for testimony, witness reliability, and degrees of legal certainty.

**Tibetan Buddhist epistemology**, building on Dignāga and Dharmakīrti, reached its mature form in the work of Sa-paṇ (Sakya Paṇḍita, 1182–1251) and the later Gelug commentarial tradition. *Tshad ma* (the Tibetan rendering of *pramāṇa*) became one of the five major fields of monastic study. Tibetan philosophers refined the typology of valid cognition into seven categories — including *yid dpyod* (correct assumption) and *snang la ma nges pa* (inattentive perception) — distinctions with no exact European parallel.

**African philosophical traditions** have been rigorously articulated since the late twentieth century. Akan (Ghanaian) epistemology, as analyzed by Kwasi Wiredu (1996), draws a fundamental distinction between *nokware* (truthfulness as moral integrity of the speaker) and *ampa* (factuality, correspondence to reality) — distinguishing two senses fused in the Indo-European concept of "truth." Henry Odera Oruka's "sage philosophy" project (Oruka, 1990) documented the systematic epistemic frameworks of indigenous Kenyan thinkers. Sub-Saharan oral traditions place strong weight on *communal testimony* as a primary source of knowledge, with reliability grounded in the relational ethics of *ubuntu*.

**Mesoamerican philosophy**, especially the Aztec/Nahua tradition reconstructed by Miguel León-Portilla (1963) and James Maffie (2014), framed knowing as a search for stable footing on a slippery earth (*tlalticpac*). The path to truth lay through *in xōchitl in cuīcatl* — "flower and song," metaphorical-poetic discourse, the only register thought capable of saying anything true on a constantly transforming earth. The figure of the *tlamatini* ("knower") integrated face (*īxtli*, the developed perspective) and heart (*yōllotl*, integrated motivation) into a unified knowing person.

## VI. Convergences and divergences

Across these traditions, three structural loci recur with striking regularity:

1. **A category of inputs or sources** — sensation, inference, testimony, and (variously admitted) revelation, intuition, or innate disposition.
2. **A category of cognitive contents** — concepts, propositions, judgments, and complex constructions built from them.
3. **A graded category of epistemic states** — doubt, opinion, belief, knowledge, certainty.

The major divergences fall into four families:

*Number and ranking of sources.* The Indian *pramāṇa* tradition canonized as many as six sources; Buddhists trimmed to two; Cārvāka materialists insisted on one. Sunni *uṣūl* recognized a similar range with a distinctive elevation of *al-tawātur* and *al-naql al-ṣaḥīḥ*. Greeks under-developed testimony as a formal source.

*Treatment of testimony and revelation.* Indian *śabda*, Sunni *al-khabar al-ṣādiq*, and Mohist *wén zhī* all admit testimony as a primary source; Cārvāka, Buddhist Dignāga, and the Greek Pyrrhonists rejected it as foundational. The Sunni and Indian *Mīmāṃsā* traditions further admit scripture as a sui generis epistemic source; the Greek and Confucian traditions do not.

*Relation of knowledge to action.* Classical Greek *epistēmē* is largely contemplative; Confucian and Wang-Yangmingian *zhī*, Mohist knowledge, and Sunni *al-ʿilm al-nāfiʿ* (beneficial knowledge) treat knowledge as completed only in right action.

*Representation versus presence.* The dominant Western and Indian models treat knowledge as a representational state — a mental content standing for its object. The Suhrawardian *ʿilm ḥuḍūrī* and certain Buddhist and Daoist registers treat the highest knowledge as non-representational presence of the object itself to consciousness.

## VII. Synthesis: a cross-civilizational taxonomy

Bringing these traditions together, we may stipulate that *epistemic content* comprises any item that can be the bearer or subject of cognition. Its principal subdivisions, abstracted from the convergent structure of the world's traditions, are:

- **Sensory inputs**: signals, stimuli, sense-data — Greek *aisthēsis*, Sunni *al-ḥiss*, Indian *pratyakṣa*, Mohist *qīn zhī*.
- **Symbolic / data-level entities**: signs, symbols, encoded data — the expression-side of the Stoic *lekton*, Mohist *míng* (名).
- **Semantic contents**: concepts, ideas, propositions, facts, information — Sunni *taṣawwur* and *qaḍiyyah*, Indian *jñāna*, Greek and Stoic *lekta*, Mohist *shí* (實).
- **Epistemic states**: doubt, opinion, belief, knowledge, certainty — Greek *doxa* and *epistēmē*, Sunni *shakk* / *ẓann* / *ʿilm* / *yaqīn*, Indian *prama* and *aprama*, Mohist *zhī*.
- **Higher-order constructs**: hypotheses, theories, laws, wisdom, insight — Greek *technē* / *phronēsis* / *sophia*, Sunni *ḥikmah* and *baṣīrah*, Indian *prajñā* and *vidyā*, Confucian *zhī* fused with *xíng* (action).

What earlier centuries treated as separate canons — *kalām*, *pramāṇa-vāda*, *Mò Jīng*, *epistēmē* — turn out, on close comparison, to map the same underlying terrain with different emphases and from different vantage points. The DIKW hierarchy popular in twentieth-century information science isolates one slice of that terrain (the ascent from raw symbols to integrated wisdom); the Indian *pramāṇa* tradition isolates the *sources* that feed into it; Sunni *kalām* isolates the *states* that occupy it; the Mohist Canon isolates the *correspondence* between language and reality that runs through all of it.

## Conclusion

Epistemic content is not a recent Western invention. It is a category that the principal civilizations of the world independently identified and articulated, each from within its own metaphysical and practical commitments. The most fertile path forward — in epistemology proper, but also in personal knowledge management, in knowledge representation systems, and in the alignment of artificial intelligence with human ways of knowing — is not to choose among these traditions but to draw them into systematic conversation. They are five longitude lines on the same globe; the territory is shared even where the maps differ.

For any modern theory of knowledge to be credible as a *human* theory rather than the parochial inheritance of a single civilization, it must be answerable to all five.
